Confirmation bias is the psychological tendency to seek out, interpret, remember, and prioritize information in a way that confirms your existing beliefs or hypotheses, while ignoring or discounting evidence that contradicts them.

## How It Operates

The bias shows up in three main stages of how we process information:

1. **Selective Search:** You only look for sources, articles, or experts that already agree with your view.
2. **Biased Interpretation:** When presented with neutral or mixed evidence, you interpret it as supporting your original position.
3. **Selective Recall:** You easily remember details that back up your view while forgetting or minimizing counter-evidence.

## Real-World Examples

- **Investing & Finance:** An investor who believes a stock will rise reads only optimistic financial analyst reports and dismisses warnings about poor company fundamentals.
- **Algorithms & Social Media:** Recommendation engines capitalize on confirmation bias by feeding users content that aligns with their historical clicks, creating echo chambers.
- [[Exploratory Data Analysis Phases|Data Analysis]]: A researcher expects a specific trend, so they focus on subsets of data that show [[Hypothesis Testing|statistical significance]]  while treating outliers or opposing signals as measurement noise.

## Strategies to Counter Confirmation Bias

- **Actively Seek Disconfirming Evidence:** Ask, _"What evidence would prove my current hypothesis wrong?"_
- **Red Teaming:** Assign someone (or play the role yourself) to argue against your primary proposal or conclusion.
- **Pre-commit Criteria:** Define what success or failure metrics look like _before_ analyzing new data or making a decision, preventing goalpost shifts later.
